# Credible commitments against blackmail

Blackmail works when complying with a threat is less costly than refusing it. A target who can change that comparison before any threat arrives can make compliance unprofitable and deter blackmailers who care about the concession.

Two different instruments can do this. A refusal-insurance policy reduces the target's loss if the threat is carried out. An anti-compliance bond increases the target's loss if they concede. The first makes refusal cheaper; the second makes compliance more expensive.

## Refusal insurance

Suppose disclosure of private information would cost the target $1 million and the blackmailer asks for $100,000. Paying can be locally rational. If an insurer credibly covers $950,000 of the disclosure loss but never reimburses the concession, refusal costs the target $50,000. The blackmailer's $100,000 demand no longer works.

This differs from ordinary kidnap, ransom, and extortion insurance. Current policies often reimburse ransoms and provide crisis-response consultants, as [Marsh's coverage summary](https://www.marsh.com/en/services/kidnap-ransom-risk-management.html) describes. That protects an insured party after an incident, but reimbursement can make payment easier. The proposed instrument covers specified harms caused by refusal while excluding payments, favors, policy changes, and other concessions.

Insurance scales beyond the target's own capital, but transfers the same problems to an underwriter. The insurer must distinguish third-party harm from losses created by the insured, price private information, survive correlated claims, and remain independent of the blackmailer. Public coverage can also attract attacks. Kidnap-and-ransom policies are often kept confidential because known coverage can increase targeting and ransom expectations ([Brockett, Golden, and Betak](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/rmir.12134)).

Deterrence can also increase realized harm. An attacker who no longer expects payment loses their financial reason to withhold the information. Insurance changes who bears the loss; it does not guarantee non-disclosure.

## Anti-compliance bonds

A target can instead lock a bond that is burned or paid to a predetermined beneficiary if evidence shows that they made a prohibited concession. If the penalty exceeds the benefit from conceding, refusal becomes the target's best response even after the threat arrives.

The bond must define the concession in observable terms. "Did not pay this address" is easier to verify than "did not secretly favor this company," "continued speaking about this issue," or "was not influenced." Secret side payments, omissions, intermediaries, and non-monetary favors are hard to exclude. A broad natural-language condition moves the whole problem into adjudication and makes false claims a second attack surface.

Paying the entire bond to whoever supplies evidence funds attempts to manufacture or acquire qualifying evidence. Burning the penalty or sending it to a fixed beneficiary removes most of that bounty, but someone still needs a bounded reward for submitting real evidence. A practical design would separate that reward from the larger penalty, require a challenge bond, and define an appeal path.

Capital is the other limit. The penalty must exceed the target's value from compliance, including harms that insurance does not cover. A wealthy or unusually damaging blackmailer can exceed the bond. Unbonding also needs a public delay, or the target can quietly exit just before conceding. During that delay, an underfunded bond can make the target worse off: the blackmailer can trigger the penalty and continue the threat.

## Commitment can deter only when it is credible

The mechanism follows the same logic as a public no-concessions policy. The United States states that it will deny hostage takers ransom, prisoner releases, policy changes, and other benefits ([Foreign Affairs Manual](https://fam.state.gov/fam/07fam/07fam1820.html)). A time-series study finds that past concessions predict additional kidnappings and supports credible no-concession policies, while noting that attackers retain other motives such as publicity ([study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0176268016300143)).

A personal bond has the same qualification. It deters only blackmailers who know about it, believe it will be enforced, and value the concession more than carrying out the threat for another reason. It cannot stop spite, publicity seeking, ideological harm, or an attacker who benefits from the threatened action itself.

The public price of refusal insurance can provide an underwriting signal. A low premium from a credible insurer indicates limited expected covered exposure. Exclusions, deductibles, insurer capital, and the scope of background checks also affect the price, so refusing a policy does not prove that someone is compromised.

## The same commitment technology strengthens attackers

Precommitment is symmetric. A blackmailer can post their own bond that is lost unless they carry out the threat after nonpayment. This removes the blackmailer's familiar credibility problem: the target can no longer hope that disclosure is costly, unpleasant, or unnecessary once payment is refused.

If the target has committed not to concede and the attacker has committed to retaliate, both commitments can work exactly as designed and force the harmful outcome. A third party can also post a bounty for private or compromising information, subsidizing the search and disclosure that refusal insurance is meant to withstand. Commitment technology changes bargaining power; it does not inherently favor the target.

## A narrow first design

A defensible pilot would:

- cover losses caused by refusal, never the concession itself;
- specify a small set of observable prohibited concessions;
- exclude losses controlled or deliberately created by the insured or claimant;
- split the evidence reward from a larger burned or preassigned penalty;
- use challenge bonds, evidence rules, and an appeal process;
- lock coverage, pricing, and withdrawal terms before any known threat; and
- publish the contract's coverage and capital without disclosing private underwriting details that help attackers choose targets.

The strongest initial use is not generic insurance for every secret. It is a public independence commitment for an officeholder, executive, custodian, or other agent whose constituency cares about a defined conflict of interest. The mechanism can make a narrow class of concessions unprofitable and visible. It cannot prove that the person is uninfluenceable, cover every harm, or prevent an attacker from making a competing commitment.

*Mechanism sketch, 11 August 2026. It has not been implemented or underwritten.*
